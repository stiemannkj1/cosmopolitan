# Plan: lua-http inside Cosmopolitan Lua

Companion to `LUA_POSIX_LFS_PLAN.md`, which supplies the luaposix and
LuaFileSystem layer this builds on.

Status. This plan has been executed. HTTP/1.1 and websockets work over
cleartext and TLS, as a client and as a server, on x86_64 and aarch64 and
on Windows. What is built, what is not, and what it cost are recorded
under "What was built" near the end, which is the section to read if you
only read one. The phases below are kept as written, because the
estimates and the reasoning are worth comparing against what actually
happened.

## Goal

`require 'http.request'` working in the fat APE, for HTTP/1.1, HTTP/2 and
websockets, over cleartext and TLS, on x86_64 and aarch64, on Linux,
macOS and Windows. No external native dependency beyond what the
monorepo already carries.

## The three dependency decisions

**lpeg stays, vendored as a C package.** It is about 4,000 lines of
dependency-free C, it compiles against cosmopolitan libc with zero errors
and zero warnings for both architectures already (see below), and keeping
it means `lpeg_patterns` drops in as 1,326 lines of untouched pure Lua.
Rewriting those RFC 3986 URI, HTTP header grammar and Set-Cookie parsers
by hand would have been days of work with real correctness risk in
security-relevant code.

**TLS comes from mbedtls, not OpenSSL.** mbedtls is already in
`third_party/mbedtls` and already does everything needed, which redbean
proves in this same repo. `tool/net/redbean.c` configures ALPN through
`mbedtls_ssl_conf_alpn_protocols`, loads the bundled CA roots through
`GetSslRoots()` from `net/https/https.h`, and keeps separate client and
server configurations. That is the template.

**cqueues gets replaced rather than vendored.** This was conditional on
whether it is a thin wrapper over a Linux API, and it is, in the way that
matters most. Its event core, `src/lib/kpoll.c`, has exactly three
backends, epoll for Linux, kqueue for the BSDs and macOS, and ports for
Solaris. There is no `poll(2)` fallback and no Windows backend anywhere
in the tree. Cosmopolitan deliberately exposes neither epoll nor kqueue,
there being no `sys/epoll.h` in `libc/isystem` and `sys/event.h` being an
empty stub whose entire contents are the comment "eventfd() is meh". Its
readiness API is `poll()` and `select()`. So vendoring cqueues means
first writing a fourth kpoll backend.

That alone would be tolerable. Two further facts settle it. cqueues'
socket layer is hard wired to OpenSSL, with `src/socket.c` and
`src/lib/socket.h` including `<openssl/ssl.h>` directly for its
`starttls`, so keeping cqueues and binding mbedtls are contradictory
goals unless its TLS layer is ported too. And cqueues is about 26,000
lines of C, most of which lua-http never touches, including a 10,000 line
DNS resolver of its own, pthread-backed threads, inotify and kqueue file
notification, and signal handling.

The subset lua-http actually uses is small, and a pure-Lua replacement
over luaposix already runs. See "Already verified".

## The architecture, in one line

Replace the dependencies, not the dependent.

lua-http is vendored byte-identical to upstream and never patched. What
gets written is a compatibility layer underneath it, published under the
module names it already requires, so that its own `require "cqueues"` and
`require "openssl.ssl.context"` resolve to the cosmo implementations.
This keeps upstream re-vendoring trivial, keeps upstream's own 5,967 line
spec suite applicable as an oracle, and confines every judgement call to
code we own.

## What gets added

Three new monorepo packages, built and registered the same way
`third_party/luaposix` and `third_party/lfs` already are, through
`scripts/luaext.py`.

`third_party/lpeg` is a C package, vendored upstream lpeg 1.1.0, with
`luaopen_lpeg` in `package.preload` under the name `lpeg`, plus upstream's
`re.lua` in the zip store.

`third_party/luahttp` is the pure-Lua payload, zipped into
`/zip/.lua/`. It holds upstream `http/*.lua` and `lpeg_patterns/*.lua`
untouched, the three pure-Lua dependencies basexx, binaryheap and fifo,
and the hand-written cqueues replacement. The shim is ours, so it lives
in the repo as source rather than being generated by the vendoring step.

`third_party/luatls` is the TLS package, a small C module over mbedtls
plus a Lua layer that shapes it into the luaossl API lua-http expects.

## Phases

**Phase 1, lpeg.** Vendor, rewrite `#include "lua.h"` in `lptypes.h` to
the repository-root form, write the `BUILD.mk` and the preload
registration, build both architectures. Half a day, and the compile step
is already proven.

**Phase 2, the pure-Lua tree.** Vendor lua-http, lpeg_patterns, basexx,
binaryheap and fifo into the zip store and confirm every module at least
loads. `compat53` is not needed, every use of it in lua-http is guarded
behind `string.pack or ...` and a `_VERSION < 5.3` test, and `zlib` is
optional behind a `pcall`. Half a day.

**Phase 3, the cqueues replacement.** The main work. Detailed below.
Three to five days.

**Phase 4, first real request.** `http.request.new_from_uri("http://...")`
against a real server, on all three targets. One day, mostly debugging
the shim against lua-http's actual expectations.

**Phase 5, TLS.** Detailed below. Four to seven days, and the one phase
where the security surface deserves care rather than speed.

**Phase 6, HTTP/2 and websockets.** h2 needs ALPN from phase 5 and
exercises the shim's condition variables hard, websockets need
`openssl.rand.bytes` for frame masking, which is worth implementing early
since it is also needed for `ws://` without any TLS at all. Two to three
days.

**Phase 7, the spec suite.** Port upstream's busted specs to a plain
runner and make them the regression gate. Two to three days.

Call it three weeks of focused work, with phases 1 through 4 being about
a week of it and delivering a working HTTP/1.1 client and server.

## Phase 3 in detail, the cqueues replacement

The surface lua-http uses was inventoried from its source rather than
guessed. From the `cqueues` module itself it uses `poll` in 30 places,
`monotime` in 8, `running` in 5, and `new`, `type` and `cancel` once
each. From `cqueues.auxlib` it uses exactly one function, `fileresult`,
in 8 places. It uses `cqueues.condition` in 5 places,
`cqueues.errno` in 10, and `cqueues.socket` in 3.

The socket object is the bulk of it, 26 distinct methods. In rough order
of how much they matter, `xread`, `xwrite`, `unget`, `fill`, `pending`,
`flush`, `setmode`, `setvbuf`, `setmaxline`, `seterror`, `clearerr`,
`error`, `onerror`, `eof`, `shutdown`, `close`, `connect`, `listen`,
`accept`, `localname`, `peername`, `pollfd`, `events`, `timeout`,
`starttls` and `checktls`.

The read modes in use are a byte count, `"*L"` for a line with its
newline kept, `"*a"` for everything to end of stream, and `"*h"` for a
header block. Reads take an optional mode string, where `"b"` means
binary, and a timeout in seconds, where 0 means do not block. Writes take
a mode string too, where `"f"` means flush now and `"n"` means do not.

The scheduler contract is the part worth stating precisely, because
lua-http relies on it in both directions. `cqueues.poll(a, b, ..., n)`
suspends the running coroutine until one of the objects becomes ready,
and returns the ones that did. A bare number among the arguments is a
timeout, and when it expires `poll` returns that number, which is how
lua-http distinguishes a timeout from readiness, as in
`if cqueues.poll(sock, timeout) ~= timeout then`. An object is pollable
when it answers `:pollfd()`, `:events()` and `:timeout()`. lua-http
implements that protocol on its own connection and server objects, in
`http/connection_common.lua` and `http/server.lua`, and passes them to
`poll` alongside sockets and condition variables, so the shim's `poll`
must accept any mix of them.

Two implementation notes that will otherwise cost a day each. SIGPIPE
must be ignored process-wide at module load, because luaposix's `send`
exposes no flags argument and so there is no `MSG_NOSIGNAL` available,
where cqueues gets this right internally. And error reporting has to
carry the errno number, not just a message, because lua-http branches on
specific values such as `EPIPE`, `ETIMEDOUT` and `ECONNRESET` throughout.

### DNS

cqueues ships its own asynchronous resolver. The shim will start with
luaposix's `getaddrinfo`, which is verified working, with one honest
consequence. It blocks, so in a single-threaded coroutine scheduler a
slow lookup stalls every other connection. That is irrelevant for a
server, which never resolves, and fine for a client doing one request at
a time. If concurrent client requests matter later, the fix is a small
asynchronous resolver written in Lua over the shim's own UDP sockets,
parsing A and AAAA records, which is a few hundred lines and fits the
architecture cleanly. Starting with `getaddrinfo` keeps phase 3 shorter
and the decision reversible.

## Phase 5 in detail, TLS

The C module should be small and should not try to look like OpenSSL. It
exposes a non-blocking TLS engine over an fd, built on
`mbedtls_ssl_set_bio` with callbacks that return
`MBEDTLS_ERR_SSL_WANT_READ` and `MBEDTLS_ERR_SSL_WANT_WRITE`, surfaced to
Lua as a tri-state of ready, want-read or want-write. The Lua socket shim
already owns the poll loop, so it drives the handshake and routes reads
and writes through the engine after `starttls`. Configuration the engine
needs to accept is the CA chain, which comes free from `GetSslRoots()`,
a certificate and key for servers, ALPN protocol lists, the verify mode,
and the expected hostname for verification.

On top of that sits a Lua layer providing the luaossl-shaped modules
lua-http requires, which are `openssl.ssl.context`, `openssl.ssl`,
`openssl.pkey`, `openssl.x509`, `openssl.x509.verify_param`,
`openssl.x509.name`, `openssl.x509.altname`, `openssl.rand`,
`openssl.digest` and `openssl.bignum`. The method surface is about 29
calls, inventoried from lua-http's source. The ones on the critical path
are `setOptions`, `setVerify`, `setStore`, `getStore`, `addDefaults`,
`setAlpnProtos`, `setAlpnSelect`, `getAlpnSelected`, `setHost`, `setIP`,
`getVerifyResult` and `getCipherInfo`.

### How the luaossl calls map

Every call on the critical path has a real mbedtls equivalent in the
version this repo carries, which is 2.26.0 in jart's fork.

`setOptions`, which lua-http uses to switch off old protocol versions
through `OP_NO_SSLv3` and friends, becomes
`mbedtls_ssl_conf_min_version` and `mbedtls_ssl_conf_max_version`.
`setGroups` and `setEphemeralKey` both become
`mbedtls_ssl_conf_curves`, since what lua-http is really expressing in
both cases is which curves are acceptable. `setAlpnProtos` becomes
`mbedtls_ssl_conf_alpn_protocols` and `getAlpnSelected` becomes
`mbedtls_ssl_get_alpn_protocol`, which is the pair HTTP/2 negotiation
depends on. `setHost` and `setIP` on a verify parameter become
`mbedtls_ssl_set_hostname`, which also drives SNI. `setVerify` becomes
`mbedtls_ssl_conf_authmode`, `getVerifyResult` becomes
`mbedtls_ssl_get_verify_result`, and `setStore` and `addDefaults` are
satisfied by `mbedtls_ssl_conf_ca_chain` over the `GetSslRoots()` bundle.

`setCipherList` turns out to be implementable rather than ignorable,
which was not obvious. jart's fork names its ciphersuites with OpenSSL
spellings rather than the usual mbedtls ones, so its table holds
`"ECDHE-ECDSA-AES256-GCM-SHA384"` where stock mbedtls would hold
`"TLS-ECDHE-ECDSA-WITH-AES-256-GCM-SHA384"`, and
`mbedtls_ssl_ciphersuite_from_string` does a plain `strcmp` against
exactly the strings lua-http passes. Two mechanical spelling differences
remain. The fork suffixes the ChaCha20 suites with `-SHA256`, and it
spells CBC out, writing `ECDHE-RSA-AES256-CBC-SHA384` where OpenSSL
writes `ECDHE-RSA-AES256-SHA384`. With a normaliser for those two rules,
lua-http's "modern" list resolves 10 suites out of 10, its
"intermediate" list 20 out of 30, and its "old" list 19 out of 32.

What does not resolve is not a translation problem. The residue is
either legacy suites this mbedtls deliberately omits, meaning static RSA
key exchange, 3DES and DSS, or it is OpenSSL cipher string *expressions*
such as `HIGH`, `AES`, `kEDH+AESGCM` and the `!aNULL` style exclusions,
which are a query language mbedtls has no equivalent for. So the rule
for the compatibility layer is to translate every concrete suite name it
recognises, ignore the expression atoms, and raise an error if a cipher
list resolves to nothing at all, so that a misconfiguration can never
quietly degrade into mbedtls' defaults.

Self-signed certificate generation is also available after all.
`third_party/mbedtls/x509write_crt.c` is vendored, so `http/server.lua`'s
convenience path through `setSubject`, `setSerial` and `setLifetime` can
be implemented rather than stubbed, though it stays low priority.

The real TLS limitation is the protocol ceiling. This mbedtls is TLS 1.2
only, with `MBEDTLS_SSL_PROTO_TLS1_3_EXPERIMENTAL` commented out in
`third_party/mbedtls/config.h`. That is fine for HTTP/2, which requires
1.2 or better, and fine for the overwhelming majority of servers, but a
1.3-only endpoint will be unreachable until cosmopolitan's mbedtls moves.
The other genuine gap is `setAlpnSelect`, a server side Lua callback for
choosing a protocol, which cannot be honoured as a callback because
mbedtls selects internally from the configured list. Configuring the list
gets the same outcome for every use lua-http makes of it.

`openssl.rand.bytes` should be implemented first and separately. It is
needed for websocket frame masking even when no TLS is involved, and it
is a few lines over `getrandom`.

## What was built

Five packages, and three edits to existing files.

`third_party/lpeg` is upstream LPeg 1.1.0, vendored, with `luaopen_lpeg`
in `package.preload` and upstream's `re.lua` in the zip store. It needed
the one include rewrite and nothing else, as predicted.

`third_party/luahttp` is the pure-Lua payload, 37 files, every one of
them upstream byte for byte: lua-http 0.4, lpeg_patterns 0.5, basexx,
binaryheap and fifo, in the zip store under `/zip/.lua/`.

`third_party/cqueues` is the replacement scheduler, about 1,100 lines of
Lua over luaposix, providing `cqueues`, `cqueues.socket`,
`cqueues.condition`, `cqueues.errno`, `cqueues.auxlib` and the TLS glue.
lua-http requires those names and gets these.

`third_party/luazlib` is a lua-zlib compatible binding over the zlib
already in the tree, which is what `http.zlib` wants.

`third_party/luatls` is the TLS support, a small C engine over mbedtls
plus the luaossl-shaped modules in Lua on top of it.

The edits are two `include` lines in the root `Makefile`, five package
names and four zip object lists in `third_party/lua/BUILD.mk`, and six
includes plus five calls in `third_party/lua/lua.main.c`.

### What the shim got wrong the first time

Every one of these was found by running lua-http against it, and each
one is worth knowing if the code is ever touched again.

`accept` does not use the same error convention as the read and write
methods. It answers a bare errno as its second return value, where
`xread` answers the `(nil, message, errno)` triple, and lua-http's
accept loop compares that second value against `ETIMEDOUT`.

A negative byte count is a non-greedy read, meaning "up to this many
bytes, as soon as there are any". lua-http opens every server connection
with one, and a shim that treats the count as a minimum spins.

The `"*h"` format reads exactly one MIME header, unfolding continuation
lines, and answers a bare nil when what comes next is not a header. That
nil is how a caller detects the blank line ending a header block, so it
has to be distinguishable from a failure.

The mode letters on `xwrite` are buffering disciplines, not flush flags.
`"f"` means fully buffered, so accumulate, and `"n"` means unbuffered, so
write through. Reading them backwards produces a server that sends its
status line and then goes quiet, which is a memorable way to spend an
afternoon.

End of file is a bare nil from `fill`, not an error. lua-http's server
tolerates exactly `nil`, `ECONNRESET`, `ENOTCONN` and `ETIMEDOUT` when
reading the next request, and treats anything else as a fault worth
reporting.

A connection has to be started eagerly, when the socket is made, because
lua-http reaches its first write with a zero timeout and expects the
handshake to already be under way.

Finishing a non-blocking connect by calling `connect()` a second time
works on Unix and does not work on Windows, where cosmopolitan answers a
win32 error rather than `EISCONN`. Waiting for writability and then
reading `SO_ERROR` is portable and is what the code does now.

Running a controller inside another controller's coroutine has to step
without blocking and then park on itself, or the outer loop never gets a
turn. lua-http's server does this on every call to `server:loop()`.

`cqueues.poll` has to block rather than yield when there is no scheduler
running, because a plain script calling `request:go()` polls from the
main thread.

### What TLS needed beyond the plan

Two things the API mapping did not anticipate.

mbedtls' contexts want stronger alignment than the body of a Lua
userdata provides. Holding an `mbedtls_ssl_config` inside a userdata
segfaults inside `mbedtls_ssl_config_defaults`. The engine now lives in
malloc'd memory with a pointer in the userdata.

lua-http asks for the curve list `P-521:P-384:P-256`, and cosmopolitan's
mbedtls has P-521 compiled out. An unsupported group id does not get
ignored, it fails the handshake with `BAD_CONFIG`. The engine now filters
the list through `mbedtls_ecp_curve_info_from_grp_id` and keeps what the
library actually has, which is the same rule the plan already set for
cipher names.

The cipher translation worked as designed. A live connection to
example.com negotiates ECDHE-ECDSA-CHACHA20-POLY1305, which is one of the
suites that only resolves because of the ChaCha20 spelling rule.

### What was verified

`scripts/luahttp_test.lua` is a 21-case suite covering the support layer
and the stack on top of it: LPeg, a URI parse through lpeg_patterns, a
zlib round trip, SHA-1 and randomness, the scheduler with two coroutines
and with a condition variable, then over loopback a GET, a POST with a
body, a 500KB body, chunked transfer encoding, gzip transfer encoding,
request headers reaching the server, two requests on one connection, and
a websocket echo. Then, against the live internet, cleartext HTTP, HTTPS
with certificate verification, and three certificates that must be
refused, being expired, self-signed, and issued for another host.

It passes 21 of 21 on all three targets, x86_64 natively, aarch64 under
`qemu-aarch64`, and Windows under `wine`. The network cases skip
themselves when there is no route out.

The three bad-certificate cases are the ones worth pointing at. A
compatibility layer over a different TLS library is exactly where
verification quietly stops happening, and these say that it has not.

Size. The x86_64 binary goes from 1,359,934 bytes with luaposix and lfs
to 1,979,389 with all of this added, and the fat APE lands at 3.8MB.

### Not built

HTTP/2 is reachable in principle, since ALPN negotiation works and the
`h2` modules load, but nothing here has exercised it against a real
HTTP/2 server, so treat it as untested rather than working. TLS 1.3 is
not available, because this mbedtls does not have it. Certificate
generation is still a stub, so lua-http's invent-a-self-signed-cert
convenience path for servers does not work and a TLS server needs a
certificate supplied. There is no public suffix list, so `http.cookie`
uses its weaker domain check. DNS still blocks the scheduler.

## Risks

The cqueues shim is the schedule risk. The surface is known and the
prototype works, but lua-http leans on exact cqueues semantics in places
the documentation does not fully pin down, particularly around what
`poll` returns when several objects are ready at once, and around the
interaction of `onerror` with buffered writes. The mitigation is phase 7
pulled earlier, using upstream's h1_connection and stream_common specs
as the oracle while the shim is still being written.

TLS is the correctness risk rather than the schedule risk, even though
the API mapping turned out to be tractable. A hand-written compatibility
layer over a different TLS library is exactly where certificate
verification quietly stops happening. The mitigation is to make
`setVerify` and hostname checking non-optional in the engine, to test
explicitly against a server with a bad certificate and assert the
failure, and to never let a verification gap be papered over by the
compatibility layer returning success for an unimplemented call. The
same discipline applies to the cipher list translation, which is why it
raises rather than falling back when nothing resolves.

HTTP/2 is the unknown. It is the part of lua-http that exercises the
scheduler hardest, through condition variables, flow control windows and
concurrent streams, and it is the part a simple shim is most likely to
get subtly wrong under load.

## Open question for later

Whether any of this should reach redbean, which has its own HTTP stack
and its own mbedtls wiring already. The two could share the TLS engine,
